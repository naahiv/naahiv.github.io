This post is intended to record some elementary proofs of properties of exterior powers, with a focus on the case of finite free modules. It is heavily inspired by Keith Conrad's notes on exterior powers. Another goal is to systematically develop the theory of determinants for finite free modules. In :post[exterior-powers-2], we will turn to vector bundles on schemes.

## Basic properties {#basic-properties}
Let $A$ be a commutative ring with $1\neq0$, and $M$ an $A$-module, and $k\geq0$ an integer. We denote by $\Alt^k(M,N)$ the $A$-module of alternating maps $M^k\to N$, i.e. $k$-multilinear maps
$$
\phi\colon M\times\cdots\times M\to N
$$
such that $\phi(x_1,\dots,x_k)=0$ whenever $x_i=x_j$ for some $i\neq j$. Of course, we can describe this as a submodule of $\Hom_A(M^{\otimes k},N)$ satisfying the same condition on elementary tensor products. Then it is obvious that $\Alt^k(M,-)\colon\Mod(A)\to\Mod(A)$ defines a functor.

:::definition
Let $M$ be an $A$-module and $k\geq0$. A $k$th exterior power of $M$ is an $A$-module $\LA^k M$ equipped with an alternating map $M^{\times k}\to\LA^k M$ making $\LA^k M$ a representing object of $\Alt^k(M,-)$.
:::

We write the universal alternating map as
$$
(x_1,\dots,x_k)\mapsto x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k,
$$
which of course factors through $M^{\otimes k}$ by definition. One easily checks that $\LA^0 M=A$ and $\LA^1 M=M$.

:::lemma
Let $M$ be an $A$-module.

1. $\LA^k M$ exists. If $\{x_i:i\in I\}$ generates $M$, then $\LA^k M$ is generated by elements of the form $x_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{i_k}$ for $i_j\in I$. If $I$ is ordered, then it is furthermore generated by such expressions where $i_1<\cdots<i_k$ in $I$.

2. We have the formula
   $$
   x_{\sigma(1)}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{\sigma(k)}=\sgn(\sigma)x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k
   $$
   for any permutation $\sigma\in S_k$ and $x_i\in M$.
:::

:::proof
We can construct it by considering the submodule $J\subset M^{\otimes k}$ generated by all elementary tensors
$$
x_1\otimes \cdots\otimes x_k
$$
such that $x_i=x_j$ for some $i\neq j$. Then take $\LA^k M= M^{\otimes k}/J$ with its natural alternating map $M^k\to M^{\otimes k}\to\LA^k M$; it is clear that this satisfies the universal property. This construction shows that the exterior power is generated by expressions as listed, and the last statement of (1) follows from (2).

To obtain (2), write $\sigma$ as a product of transpositions $\tau_r\cdots\tau_1$ and induct on $r$. In the base case, $\sigma=\tau_1=(ij)$, and then
$$
\begin{aligned}
0&=x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge(x_i+x_j)\wedge\cdots\wedge(x_i+x_j)\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k \\
&=x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_i\wedge\cdots\wedge x_j\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k \\
&\quad+x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_j\wedge\cdots\wedge x_i\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k
\end{aligned}
$$
gives the result. In the general case, induction implies
$$
x_{\sigma(1)}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{\sigma(k)}=(-1)^{r-1}x_{\tau_r(1)}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{\tau_r(k)},
$$
and then the base case applied to the RHS completes the proof.
:::

A simple corollary is that if $M$ is generated by $m$ elements, then $\LA^k M=0$ for $k>m$ by the pigeonhole principle. It is easy to see that $\LA^k$ defines a functor $\Mod(A)\to\Mod(A)$, where given $\phi\colon M\to N$, we define $\LA^k\phi$ to be the unique linear map satisfying
$$
\LA^k\phi(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k)=\phi(x_1)\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi(x_k),
$$
and functoriality follows from uniqueness in the universal property right away. In general, there is an important map
$$
\Emb^k_M\colon\LA^k M\to M^{\otimes k}
$$
defined as the unique linear map extending
$$
\Emb^k_M(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_k}\sgn(\sigma)x_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{\sigma(k)}.
$$
To check this is well-defined, suppose $x_i=x_j$ for some $i\neq j$; then the RHS above becomes
$$
\sum_{[\sigma]\in \langle(ij)\rangle\backslash S_k}\big(\sgn(\sigma)x_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{\sigma(k)}+\sgn((ij)\sigma)x_{(ij)\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{(ij)\sigma(k)}\big)
$$
by summing over coset representatives. The assumption implies that $x_{(ij)\sigma(m)}=x_{\sigma(m)}$ for all $m$, and $\sgn((ij)\sigma)=-\sgn(\sigma)$. By linearity, it is easy to check that $\Emb^k$ is a natural transformation from $\LA^k\to\bigotimes^k$. The following is the only real theorem about exterior powers.

:::theorem{#thm:free-basis}
Let $M$ be a free $A$-module with a basis $\{e_i:i\in I\}$ for an ordered index set $I$. Then $\LA^k M$ is free for all $k$ with basis
$$
\{e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_k}:i_1<\cdots<i_k\in I\}.
$$
In this case, $\Emb_M^k$ is a monomorphism.
:::

:::proof
Let $I_<^k=\{(i_1,\dots,i_k)\in I^k:i_1<\cdots<i_k\}$, then we can write an arbitrary element of $\LA^k M$ as
$$
\omega=\sum_{\alpha\in I_<^k}c_\alpha e_\alpha^{\wedge}
$$
where $e_\alpha^{\wedge}=e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_k}$ if $\alpha=(i_1,\dots,i_k)$. If $\Emb(\omega)=0$, then
$$
0=\sum_{\sigma\in S_k}\sum_{\alpha\in I^k_<}\sgn(\sigma)c_\alpha e_{\sigma(\alpha)}^{\otimes}
$$
where $e^\otimes_\alpha=e_{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_k}$ and $\sigma(\alpha)=(i_{\sigma(1)},\dots,i_{\sigma(k)})$. Note that the action map $S_k\times I^k_<\to I^k$ is injective, and each of the elements $e^{\otimes}_{\sigma(\alpha)}$ are basis elements of $M^{\otimes k}$ with respect to the induced basis, thus $\sgn(\sigma)c_\alpha=0$, i.e. $\omega=0$ and $\Emb$ is injective. In particular, if $\omega=0$, then $\Emb(\omega)=0$ and the above showed that $c_\alpha=0$ for each $\alpha$, proving linear independence.
:::

:::corollary
Let $M$ be an $A$-module.

1. If $M$ is projective, then $\LA^k M$ is projective for all $k$, and the map $\Emb_M^k\colon\LA^k M\to M^{\otimes k}$ is a monomorphism.

2. If $\phi\colon M\to N$ is a surjection (resp. isomorphism), then $\LA^k\phi$ is a surjection (isomorphism) for all $k$.

3. If $\phi\colon M\to N$ is a monomorphism, $M$ is projective, and $N$ is flat, then $\LA^k\phi$ is a monomorphism for all $k$.
:::

:::proof
(1) If $i\colon M\to F$ is a split monomorphism to a free module, then $\LA^k i$ is also a split monomorphism into a free module, using :ref[thm:free-basis]. By naturality of $\Emb^k$, the second statement follows since $\LA^k M\to\LA^k F\to F^{\otimes k}$ are both monomorphisms.

(2) These are obvious from the discussions above.

(3) Naturality of $\Emb$ and (1) reduce the problem to showing that $M^{\otimes k}\to N^{\otimes k}$ is injective. Inductively, if $M^{\otimes (k-1)}\to N^{\otimes(k-1)}$ is injective then both maps in the composite
$$
M^{\otimes k}\to N^{\otimes(k-1)}\otimes M\to N^{\otimes(k-1)}\otimes N=N^{\otimes k}
$$
are injective, first by flatness of $M$ and then flatness of $N^{\otimes(k-1)}$.
:::

:::corollary
1. Let $\phi\colon A^m\to A^n$ be a map. If $\phi$ is injective (resp. surjective) then $m\leq n$ (resp. $m\geq n$).

2. If $M$ is free and $\{x_1,\dots, x_m\}$ is a linearly independent subset, then
   $$
   \{x_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{i_k}:1\leq i_1<\cdots<i_k\leq m\}.
   $$
   is a linearly independent subset of $\LA^k M$.
:::

:::proof
(1) follows from applying $\LA^m$ or $\LA^n$ to the map and observing that one of these is zero. (2) follows from the above by looking at the embedding $A^m\to M$ corresponding to the linearly independent subset.
:::

:::corollary
Let $M$ be a free module. Then a subset $\{x_1,\dots,x_m\}$ is linearly dependent if and only if $x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_m$ is a torsion element of $\LA^m M$. Here, torsion means annihilated by some nonzero element of $A$.
:::

:::proof
If $\{x_1,\dots,x_m\}$ is linearly independent, the singleton $\{x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_m\}$ is linearly independent by the above, hence this is torsion free. On the other hand, if there is a relation
$$
a_1 x_1+\cdots + a_m x_m=0
$$
in $M$ with $a_i\neq0$, then
$$
0=x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\sum\nolimits_j a_j x_j\wedge\cdots\wedge x_m=a_i(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_m),
$$
as desired.
:::


### Determinants {#determinants}

Let $M$ be a finite free module of rank $n$. Then :ref[thm:free-basis] shows that $\LA^n M$ is free of rank 1. Thus, if $\phi\colon M\to M$ is an endomorphism, then $\LA^n\phi$ is multiplication by a unique element of $A$, which we write as $\det{\phi}\in A$ and call the **determinant** of $\phi$.

:::lemma
Let $\phi,\psi\colon M\to M$ be endomorphisms of a finite free module $M$. Then $\det(\phi\circ\psi)=\det(\phi)\det(\psi)$. In particular, if $\phi$ is an automorphism then $\det(\phi)\in A^\times$.
:::

:::proof
The second statement follows immediately, and the first is a simple consequence of functoriality of $\LA^n$.
:::


We will revisit determinants in more detail once we have discussed the wedge product and duality of exterior powers. For now, we state a few basic computations.

:::lemma
Let $\phi\colon M\to M$ be an endomorphism of a finite free module $M$, $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ be a basis of $M$, and $\phi(e_j)=\sum_i a_{ij}e_i$ for all $j$. Then
$$
\det(\phi)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\sgn(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1)1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)n}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\sgn(\sigma)a_{1\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{n\sigma(n)}.
$$
In particular, $\det(\phi)=\det(\phi^\lor)$, where $\phi^\lor\colon M^\lor\to M^\lor$ is the dual map.
:::

:::proof
The second equality follows from the first because we can sum over $\sigma^{-1}\in S_n$, use $\sgn(\sigma)=\sgn(\sigma^{-1})$, and the fact that
$$
a_{\sigma^{-1}(1)1}\cdots a_{\sigma^{-1}(n)n}=a_{1\sigma(1)}\cdots a_{n\sigma(n)}
$$
by simply permuting the product. For the first, we observe
$$
\begin{aligned}
    \det(\phi)e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n &= \sum_{i_1}a_{i_11}e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\sum_{i_n}a_{i_n n}e_{i_n} \\
    &=\sum_{i_1,\dots,i_n} a_{i_1 1}\cdots a_{i_n n} e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_n} \\
    &=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}a_{\sigma(1)1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)n}e_{\sigma(1)}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{\sigma(n)} \\
    &=\big(\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\sgn(\sigma)a_{\sigma(1)1}\cdots a_{\sigma(n)n}\big)e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n,
\end{aligned}
$$
where in the third equality we are using the fact that the action map
$$
S_n\to\{1,\dots,n\}^n\qquad\sigma\mapsto(\sigma(1),\dots\sigma(n))
$$
identifies $S_n$ with the subset of the codomain consisting of tuples with no repetition, and also that any terms in the second sum with repetition must vanish.

The statement about $\phi^\lor$ follows from the fact that if $\{\varepsilon^1,\dots,\varepsilon^n\}$ is the dual basis, then
$$
\phi^\lor(\varepsilon^i)(e_j)=\varepsilon^i(\phi(e_j))=a_{ij},
$$
i.e. $\phi^\lor(\varepsilon^i)=\sum_j a_{ij}\varepsilon^j$, so applying the first part to $\phi^\lor$ in this basis gives the second expression in the lemma statement, completing the proof.
:::

We recall that an $m\times n$ matrix in $A$ is an element of $(A^m)^n$, thought of as an array with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. If $X$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, then we write $X=(a_{ij})_{ij}$ to denote the components of $X$, where $a_{ij}$ is the $i$th row and $j$th column. An $m\times n$ matrix is equivalently an element of $\Hom_A(A^n,A^m)$, where a map $\phi$ corresponds to a matrix with components described by $\phi(e_j)=\sum_i a_{ij}e_i$.

We also recall that if $X=(a_{ij})$ is an $m\times n$ matrix and $Y=(b_{kl})$ is an $n\times r$ matrix, then their product $XY$ is the $m\times r$ matrix corresponding to the composite map $A^r\to A^n\to A^m$. In components, $XY=(c_{ij})$ then
$$
c_{ik}=\sum_j a_{ij}b_{jk}.
$$
The $A$-module of $m\times n$ matrices is denoted $M_{m\times n}(A)$, and the special case of $n\times n$ is denoted $M_n(A)$. The **determinant** of an $n\times n$ matrix is its determinant as a map $A^n\to A^n$.

:::proposition
Let $n\geq0$ and $X,Y\in M_n(A)$.

1. $\det(XY)=\det(X)\det(Y)$ and $\det(\Id)=1$. If $Y$ is an invertible matrix, then $\det(Y^{-1}XY)=\det(X)$.

2. $\det(X)=\det(X^{\intercal})$, where $X^\intercal$ denotes the transpose of $X$.

3. $\det(X)$ is annihilated by a nonzero element of $A$ if and only if the columns of $X$ are linearly dependent, if and only if the rows of $X$ are linearly dependent.

4. If $Y$ is obtained from $X$ by scaling a single row or column by $a\in A$, then $\det(Y)=a\det(X)$. In particular, $\det(aX)=a^n\det(X)$.

5. If $Y$ is obtained from $X$ by permuting the rows or columns by $\sigma\in S_n$, then $\det(Y)=\sgn(\sigma)\det(X)$.

6. If any row (column, resp.) of $X$ is a scalar multiple of another row (column), then $\det(X)=0$.

7. For $v_1,\dots,v_n\in A^n$, we have
   $$
   \det(v_1,\dots,v_i+w,\dots,v_n)=\det(v_1,\dots,v_n)+\det(v_1,\dots,w,\dots,v_n)
   $$
   for any $w\in A^n$. The corresponding statement for rows holds as well.
:::

:::proof
All of these statements are immediate for us either because of one of the previous two lemmas, or by the corresponding properties of the exterior product.
:::

## The exterior product {#exterior-product}
Let $M$ be an $A$-module and $k,l\geq0$. Then we have an alternating map
$$
M^k\to\Alt^l(M,\LA^{k+l}M)
$$
defined by sending $(x_1,\dots,x_k)$ to the alternating map
$$
(y_1,\dots,y_l)\mapsto x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k\wedge y_1\wedge\cdots\wedge y_l.
$$
Thus we obtain a bilinear map
$$
\wedge\colon\LA^k M\otimes_A\LA^l M\to\LA^{k+l}M
$$
which we denote $(\omega,\eta)\mapsto\omega\wedge\eta$. From our description of generators, it is easy to see that this is the unique bilinear map satisfying
$$
(x_1\wedge\dots\wedge x_k,y_1\wedge\cdots\wedge y_l)\mapsto x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k\wedge y_1\wedge\cdots\wedge y_l.
$$
We note that if $k$ or $l$ is zero, then this is just scalar multiplication.

:::proposition
Let $M$ be an $A$-module and $j,k,l\geq0$.

1. $(\omega\wedge\eta)\wedge\xi=\omega\wedge(\eta\wedge\xi)$ for $\omega\in\LA^j M,\eta\in\LA^k M,\xi\in\LA^lM$.

2. $\omega\wedge\eta=(-1)^{jk}\eta\wedge\omega$ for $\omega\in\LA^j M,\eta\in\LA^k M$.

In particular, $\LA M\coloneqq\bigoplus_{i=0}^\infty\LA^iM$ forms a graded-commutative $A$-algebra with the product $\wedge$ extended by linearity, called the **exterior algebra** of $M$.
:::

:::proof
(1) The two ways to parenthesize give two multilinear maps
$$
\LA^j M\times\LA^kM\times\LA^lM\to\LA^{j+k+l}M,
$$
and to check equality we can check on elementary wedge products, in which case the result is obvious from the definition.

(2) After reducing to the elementary wedge products, the question reduces to computing the sign of the permutation
$$
(1,\dots,j,j+1,\dots,j+k)\mapsto(j+1,\dots,j+k,1,\dots,j).
$$
Indeed, going in order it takes $j$ transpositions to move $j+i$ to the $i$th spot, and in total this is $j+\cdots+j=jk$ transpositions.
:::

:::lemma
If $M$ is generated by $\{x_1,\dots,x_m\}$, then
$$
\LA^kM=\sum_{i=1}^m\big(\LA^{k-1}M\big)\wedge x_i.
$$
for all $k\geq1$.
:::

:::proof
We know that the elements $x_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_{i_k}$ generate $\LA^kM$ for $i_1<\cdots<i_k$, and by incurring a sign we can always move any element in the wedge to the end, giving the representation above.
:::

:::proposition
Let $M$ be an $A$-module and $\omega\in\LA^kM$ for $k$ odd. Then $\omega\wedge\omega=0$.
:::

:::proof
We use standard multi-index notation. If $\omega=\sum_I c_I x_I$, then
$$
\omega\wedge\omega=\sum_{I,J}c_Ic_Jx_I\wedge x_J=\sum_{I\neq J}c_Ic_J x_I\wedge x_J,
$$
and for each term $c_Ic_J x_I\wedge x_J$ with $I\neq J$ there is a unique corresponding term $c_Ic_J x_J\wedge x_I$, but now we are done because
$$
x_I\wedge x_J+x_J\wedge x_I=\big(1+(-1)^{k^2}\big)x_I\wedge x_J=0
$$
as $k$ was assumed odd.
:::

Now let us briefly discuss some easy properties of the exterior algebra, in particular its rank, a duality result, and a Künneth formula. First, if $M$ is finite free with basis $\{e_1,\dots,e_m\}$, then we know that the algebra $\LA M$ is finite free with basis
$$
\{e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_k}:0\leq k\leq m, i_1<\cdots<i_k\}.
$$
The number of such elements is the same as the number of subsets of $\{1,\dots,m\}$ ($\varnothing$ corresponds to $1\in\LA^0M=A$), so the rank of $\LA M$ is $2^m$. Next, we observe that the formation of the full exterior algebra $\LA$ is also functorial because of the following naturality property, for $\phi\colon M\to N$
$$
\begin{CD}
\LA^kM\otimes_A\LA^lM @>{\wedge}>> \LA^{k+l}M \\
@V{\LA^k\phi\otimes\LA^l\phi}VV @VV{\LA^{k+l}\phi}V \\
\LA^kN\otimes_A\LA^lN @>{\wedge}>> \LA^{k+l}N
\end{CD}
$$
for which commutativity is easy to check on elementary wedge products.

### Duality and the adjugate {#duality-and-adjugate}

Next we discuss duality. Recall that a bilinear pairing between modules $M$ and $N$ normally means a bilinear map $M\times N\to A$, and it is **perfect** if the induced maps
$$
M\to\Hom_A(N,A)=N^\lor\qquad\text{and}\qquad N\to M^\lor
$$
are isomorphisms. Here we slightly generalize this definition so that $A$ is allowed to be any rank 1 free module $L$, and perfectness means that the maps to $\Hom(N,L)$ and $\Hom(M,L)$ are isomorphisms. In particular, when $N$ is finite free, the statement applies to $M\isoto N^\lor\otimes_A L$.

:::proposition
Let $M$ be a finite free module of rank $n$. Then the wedge product
$$
\LA^k M\times\LA^{n-k}M\to\LA^n M
$$
forms a perfect pairing for $0\leq k\leq n$.
:::

:::proof
The question is well-posed since $\LA^nM$ is a free module of rank 1. If $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ is a basis, then for $I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}$ a subset of size $k$, we can uniquely write $I=\{i_1,\dots,i_k\}$ for $i_1<\cdots<i_k$, so write $e_I=e_{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_k}$; the same thing can be done for $n-k$. We have a bijection
$$
\{I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}:\abs{I}=k\}\isoto \{I\subset\{1,\dots,n\}:\abs{I}=n-k\}
$$
given by $I\mapsto\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus I$, which induces a bijection between the bases for $\LA^kM$ and $\LA^{n-k}M$. By construction,
$$
e_I\wedge e_{\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus I}=\pm e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n\coloneqq(-1)^{a_I}e_{\{1,\dots,n\}}
$$
for some numbers $a_I$. On the other hand, if $J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}$ is of size $n-k$ and $I\cap J\neq\varnothing$, then $e_I\wedge e_J=0$; it follows that the bases
$$
\{e_I:I\subset\{1,\dots,n\},\abs{I}=k\}\qquad \{(-1)^{a_I}e_{\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus I}:I\subset\{1,\dots,n\},\abs{I}=k\}
$$
of $\LA^kM$ and $\LA^{n-k}M$ are dual under the pairing.
:::

As a corollary of this, whenever $M$ is free of rank $n\geq1$, for each map $\phi\colon M\to M$ there is an **adjugate map** $\Adj(\phi)$ defined by commutativity of the following diagram.
```tikzcd {#diag:adjugate description="The perfect wedge pairing identifies M with Hom from its (n minus 1)st exterior power to its top exterior power. The dashed adjugate map corresponds to precomposition by the (n minus 1)st exterior power of phi."}
\begin{tikzcd}[sep=scriptsize]
	M & {\Hom_A(\LA^{n-1}M,\LA^{n}M)} \\
	M & {\Hom_A(\LA^{n-1}M,\LA^{n}M)}
	\arrow["\cong", from=1-1, to=1-2]
	\arrow["{\Adj(\phi)}"', dashed, from=1-1, to=2-1]
	\arrow["{(\LA^{n-1}\phi)^*}", from=1-2, to=2-2]
	\arrow["\cong", from=2-1, to=2-2]
\end{tikzcd}
```
Explicitly, $\Adj(\phi)$ is the unique map $M\to M$ such that
$$
\Adj(\phi)(x)\wedge\eta=x\wedge\phi(\eta)\qquad\eta\in\LA^{n-1}M.
$$
Observe that for each $\eta$, we have
$$
\Adj(\phi)\phi(x)\wedge\eta=\phi(x)\wedge\phi(\eta)=\phi(x\wedge\eta)=\det(\phi)x\wedge\eta.
$$
By non-degeneracy of the pairing, $\Adj(\phi)(\phi(x))=\det(\phi)x$, i.e. we have the relation $\Adj(\phi)\circ\phi=\det(\phi)\Id_M$. This gives an easy criterion for invertibility:

:::theorem
Let $M$ be a finite free module of positive rank and $\phi\colon M\to M$ a map. The following are equivalent:

1. $\phi$ is an isomorphism.

2. $\phi$ is surjective.

3. $\det(\phi)\in A^\times$.

In this situation, $\det(\phi)^{-1}\Adj(\phi)$ is the inverse of $\phi$.
:::

:::proof
If $(2)$ holds, then by freeness $\phi$ is a split epimorphism, so multiplicativity of the determinant shows that $\det(\phi)$ is a unit.

If $(3)$ holds, then $\Adj(\phi)$ has a right inverse $\det(\phi)^{-1}\phi$ and is thus surjective, while similarly $\phi$ has a left inverse $\det(\phi)^{-1}\Adj(\phi)$ and is therefore injective. Then $\det(\Adj(\phi))$ is also a unit, so the same argument replacing $\phi$ with $\Adj(\phi)$ shows that $\Adj(\phi)$ is also injective. Thus $\Adj(\phi)$ and its right inverse are both isomorphisms, so $\phi$ is an isomorphism.
:::

For the zero module, the three conditions hold as well: its unique endomorphism is an isomorphism and has determinant 1.

:::remark
We briefly note that the adjugate as defined is of course the same thing as defined in linear algebra, and we can extract the minors and cofactor matrices from the exterior algebra as well. It is an easy exercise to compare this with the classical definition and obtain these (and e.g. the Laplace expansion) by standard properties of the wedge products.
:::

:::remark
In the above situation, observe that if $\det(\phi)$ is not a zero divisor in $A$ (which we have shown happens iff $\phi$ maps any basis to a linearly independent set), then taking determinants and cancelling $\det(\phi)$ shows that
$$
\det(\Adj(\phi))=\det(\phi)^{n-1}.
$$
In rank 1, $\Adj(\phi)=\Id_M$. Thus $\Adj(\phi)$ can be an automorphism while $\phi$ is injective but non-surjective: multiplication by 2 on $\ZZ$ is an example.
:::

:::corollary
Let $M$ be a finite free module of rank $n$. Then $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ is a basis for $M$ if and only if $x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_n$ is a basis for $\LA^nM$, i.e. if it spans $\LA^nM$ over $A$.
:::

:::proof
One direction is known; for the other, construct a map $\phi\colon M\to M$ to send a basis of $M$ to $x_1,\dots,x_n$. Taking top exterior powers, a basis of $\LA^nM$ is mapped to $x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_n$ which by assumption is an isomorphism, so $\det(\phi)\in A^\times$ which completes the proof.
:::

### The exterior algebra and direct sums {#exterior-algebra}

Now we conclude this section with some more basic properties of the exterior algebra on a module.

:::proposition
Let $M$ be an $A$-module. For any central $A$-algebra $B$ and a map $\psi\colon M\to B$ of $A$-modules for which $\psi(x)^2=0$ for all $x\in M$, there is a unique extension $\wt{\psi}\colon\LA(M)\to B$ of $\psi$ to a map of $A$-algebras.
:::

:::proof
Note that uniqueness is clear, because as an $A$-algebra $\LA(M)$ is generated by its submodule $M$. For any $x,y\in M$, it follows from the assumption
$$
0=\psi(x+y)^2=\psi(x)\psi(y)+\psi(y)\psi(x),
$$
so any product as above can be permuted by incurring a sign. Thus for any $x_1,\dots,x_k\in M$, $\psi(x_1)\cdots\psi(x_k)=0$ if $x_i=x_j$ for $i\neq j$, since the terms can be brought together by a permutation.

This implies that $(x_1,\dots,x_k)\mapsto\psi(x_1)\cdots\psi(x_k)$ is an alternating map $M^k\to B$, and thus it provides a map $\LA^kM\to B$ for each $k\geq0$. These all glue into a map $\wt{\psi}\colon\LA(M)\to B$ of $A$-modules, and it is obvious that the multiplicative structure is respected, because
$$
\wt{\psi}(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k)=\psi(x_1)\cdots\psi(x_k)=\wt{\psi}(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_i)\wt{\psi}(x_{i+1}\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k)
$$
for any $i\leq k$.
:::


We remark that the above proposition is the universal property of $\LA(M)$ as a central $A$-algebra, but not as a graded-commutative ring. For this, we have an upgraded picture.

:::lemma
Let $M$ be an $A$-module. For each graded-commutative $A$-algebra $B$ and $A$-linear map $\psi\colon M\to B_1$ such that $\psi(x)^2=0$ for all $x\in M$, there is a unique graded map $\wt{\psi}\colon\LA(M)\to B$ such that $\wt{\psi}\rvert_M=\psi$.
:::

:::proof
The situation in the proposition above still applies, so we just need to check that the resulting map $\wt{\psi}$ is graded. Indeed, this is demonstrated by the rule
$$
\wt{\psi}(x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k)=\psi(x_1)\cdots\psi(x_k),
$$
which shows that $\LA^kM$ is mapped into the degree $k$ component of $B$.
:::

Here is the application: recall that in the category of graded-commutative $A$-algebras, the coproduct coincides with the graded tensor product where we define $B\otimes_A C$ by
$$
(B\otimes_A C)^n=\bigoplus_{i+j=n}B^i\otimes_A C^j
$$
on the underlying modules, and the multiplication is given by
$$
(a\otimes b)(a'\otimes b')=(-1)^{\abs{b}\abs{a'}}aa'\otimes bb'.
$$
In this sense, we will show that the exterior algebra turns direct sums into graded tensor products. The simple tool is the functor
$$
\scr{N}^1\colon\operatorname{Alg}_{\mathrm{gr}}(A)\to\Mod(A),
$$
defined by $\scr{N}^1(B)=\{x\in B^1:x^2=0\}$. Note that if $x,y\in\scr{N}^1(B)$, then
$$
(x+y)^2=x^2+xy+yx+y^2=xy+yx=0
$$
because $x$ and $y$ are degree 1; thus this is an $A$-module. It is clear that graded maps restrict to this degree 1 submodule to make this functorial.

:::theorem
There is a fully faithful adjunction
$$
\LA\colon\Mod(A)\rightleftarrows\operatorname{Alg}_{\mathrm{gr}}(A)\,:\scr{N}^1
$$
with unit the canonical isomorphism $M\isoto\scr{N}^1(\LA M)$. In particular, for any $A$-modules $M$ and $N$ there is a natural isomorphism
$$
\LA(M\oplus N)\isoto\LA(M)\otimes_A\LA(N)
$$
to the graded tensor product, and thus an isomorphism
$$
\LA^n(M\oplus N)\isoto\bigoplus_{i+j=n}\LA^iM\otimes_A\LA^jN
$$
of $A$-modules for all $n\geq0$.
:::

:::proof
The latter statements reduce to proving the adjunction, since $\oplus$ is the coproduct in $\Mod(A)$, yet the previous lemma proves the adjunction. It's fully faithful because each element in $M\subset\LA(M)$ squares to zero.
:::

:::example{#example:splitting-of-modules}
Here is an important example of this: Let $M_1,\dots,M_k$ be a sequence of $A$-modules generated by $n_1,n_2,\dots,n_k$ elements respectively. Then their direct sum is generated by $n\coloneqq n_1+\cdots+n_k$ elements, and we have
$$
\LA^{n}\Big(\bigoplus_{i=1}^k M_i\Big)\cong\bigotimes_{i=1}^k\LA^{n_i}M_i.
$$
To prove this, we observe that the statement is true if we remove the $n$ and $n_i$, and then observe that in any sum $n=j_1+\cdots+j_k$ if $j_i= n_i$ doesn't hold for all $i$, then $j_i>n_i$ for some $i$, so $\LA^{j_i}M_i=0$. Of course, in the finite free case one could count ranks to verify this result, but the importance is that there is a canonical isomorphism. This will be used in :post[exterior-powers-2].
:::

### The transpose pairing {#transpose-pairing}

We prove one final duality result in this section, which relates dual modules to the exterior algebra. For an $A$-module $M$, we have a **transpose pairing**
$$
\LA^k M^\lor\times\LA^k M\to A
$$
defined as the unique bilinear pairing such that
$$
(\varphi_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\varphi_k,x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k)\mapsto\det([\varphi_i(x_j)]_{ij}),
$$
defined using the matrix determinant from :ref[basic-properties]. It is easy to verify that the corresponding map $(M^\lor)^k\to\Alt^k(M,A)$ is well-defined using properties that we have proven of the determinant, thus the above map is well-defined.

:::proposition
Let $M$ be a finite free module. Then the transpose/determinant pairing
$$
\det\colon\LA^k M^\lor\times\LA^k M\to A
$$
is a perfect pairing for all $k\geq0$.
:::

:::proof
For $k=0$, the pairing is multiplication $A\times A\to A$. Assume $k>0$. Let $\{e_1,\dots,e_n\}$ be a basis of $M$ and write $\{\delta^1,\dots,\delta^n\}$ for the dual basis, and consider the corresponding bases of $\LA^kM^\lor$ and $\LA^kM$ corresponding to subsets $I,J\subset\{1,\dots,n\}$ of size $k$ respectively. If $I=J$, then
$$
\det(\delta^I,e_I)=\det([\delta^{i_{l}}(e_{i_m})]_{lm})=\det([\delta_{lm}]_{lm})=\det(\Id)=1.
$$
On the other hand, suppose $I\neq J$: if $i_q\in I\setminus J$, then the row
$$
\delta^{i_q}(e_{j_1}),\quad\delta^{i_q}(e_{j_2}),\quad\dots\quad,\delta^{i_q}(e_{j_k})
$$
in the corresponding matrix is identically zero, so $\det(\delta^I,e_J)=0$; we have a similar outcome if some $j_q\in J\setminus I$. This shows that these two bases are dual with respect to the determinant pairing.
:::

:::corollary
For a finite free module $M$, the natural determinant map
$$
\LA(M^\lor)\to\LA(M)^\lor
$$
is an isomorphism of graded $A$-modules, where the dual on the right is taken degreewise.
:::

We note that the determinant pairing can also be described in the following way. The matrix determinant is best described as the inverse map $\LA^n A^n\to A$ to the isomorphism $A\to\LA^n A^n$ sending $1\mapsto e_1\wedge\cdots\wedge e_n$. Each evaluation map $M^\lor\times M\to A$ induces
$$
\LA^k M^\lor\times\LA^k M\to\LA^k A^k
$$
which can be defined in two ways: either it is defined as
$$
(\lambda_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\lambda_k,x_1\wedge\cdots\wedge x_k)\mapsto\big(\sum_i\lambda_1(x_i)e_i\big)\wedge\cdots\wedge\big(\sum_i\lambda_k(x_i)e_i\big),
$$
or the same expression but where we sum over $\lambda$'s instead of $x$'s. To check well-definedness from this definition, one needs the equivalence of these two definitions, which follows from the transpose invariance of determinant proven in :ref[basic-properties]. There is also a question of naturality: given a map $\phi\colon M\to N$ between finite free $A$-modules, the diagram
$$
\begin{CD}
\LA^kN^\lor @>{\cong}>> (\LA^kN)^\lor \\
@V{\LA^k(\phi^\lor)}VV @VV{(\LA^k\phi)^\lor}V \\
\LA^kM^\lor @>{\cong}>> (\LA^kM)^\lor
\end{CD}
$$
indeed commutes, because the second square in the diagram
$$
\begin{CD}
\LA^kN^\lor @>>> \Hom(\LA^kN,\LA^k A^k) @>{\det}>> (\LA^kN)^\lor \\
@V{\LA^k(\phi^\lor)}VV @VV{(\LA^k\phi)^*}V @VV{(\LA^k\phi)^\lor}V \\
\LA^kM^\lor @>>> \Hom(\LA^kM,\LA^k A^k) @>{\det}>> (\LA^kM)^\lor
\end{CD}
$$
commutes for standard reasons, and the first is easy to check by either of the equivalent definitions above.
